UNIT B · THE TECHNOLOGY STACK — LESSON 0013

Named vs. real practice

AI-DLC isn't the only named methodology for building software alongside agents — and the honest evidence suggests most teams aren't using a named one at all.

AI-DLC — AWS's named methodology

AI-DLC ("AI-Driven Development Lifecycle") runs on a unit called a Bolt — the smallest iteration across three phases (Inception, Construction, Operations), sized in hours or days rather than a fixed two-week sprint.[1] Its two signature ceremonies, Mob Elaboration and Mob Construction, explicitly borrow from Woody Zuill's mob-programming practice (the whole team working the same task, same time, same space): AI drafts requirements or code, the whole cross-functional team validates live, compressing what traditionally takes weeks into hours. The stated core philosophy is "AI proposes, human approves," with gates at every stage.[1]

read the adoption numbers skeptically The specific case studies cited for AI-DLC — Wipro compressing ~3 months of planned work into ~20 hours, a fintech building an app in 48 hours, "10–15x productivity" — all trace back to AWS's own re:Invent 2025 session and blog posts, based on "100+ customer experiments" AWS itself ran or co-ran. No independently audited case study exists yet. Treat these like any vendor benchmark: real, but not neutral.

The competing (and non-competing) framings

AI-DLC is a real, named, AWS-specific artifact — but it's one of several, and some of the most credible names in software deliberately chose not to name one at all. Worth surveying honestly rather than treating AI-DLC as the default:

Vendor / orgWhat they actually did
AWSNamed methodology (AI-DLC): phases, Bolts, Mob Elaboration/Construction, reference tooling (Kiro, Amazon Q, Strands, AgentCore).
GitHubSpec Kit — an open-source, vendor-agnostic toolkit for Spec-Driven Development: a repeatable specify → plan → tasks → implement workflow via CLI, working across 30+ coding agents (not just Copilot). 111k GitHub stars — arguably the most credible AI-DLC peer, since it's genuinely vendor-neutral by design.[2]
Microsoft"Agentic DevOps" — looser than it sounds: no fixed ceremony structure like Bolts. Operationalized mainly as a partner certification track ("Agentic DevOps with Microsoft Azure and GitHub"), not an open workflow spec.[3]
ThoughtworksAgent/works, "3-3-3" — concept in 3 days, functional prototype in 3 weeks, production-ready MVP in 3 months. A genuine named consultancy methodology, distinct from AWS's.[4]
Atlassian, GitLabExplicitly chose not to brand a methodology. Atlassian ships principles ("structured intent," "observable autonomy") and product features (Jira Planner, Coding Agent) instead of a ceremony framework; GitLab's Duo Agent Platform (GA Jan 2026) embeds agents as platform capabilities, same pattern.[5]
Google CloudInvestment/ecosystem-focused (~$750M partner fund for systems integrators), not a competing named workflow.

One more data point on how unsettled this still is: Forrester's Top 10 Emerging Technologies for 2026 names "agentic software development" as a distinct emerging category — but explicitly says it's 2–5 years from delivering significant benefit, and there's no Gartner Magic Quadrant or Forrester Wave yet treating "agentic SDLC" as a ratable market. The analysts who normally certify "this is now a category" haven't done so.[6]

What teams are actually doing

Five independent 2026 surveys converge on the same honest picture, and it's not "teams are adopting a named methodology":

  • Stack Overflow 2026: AI tool adoption hit a record 84% — but 52% of developers don't use agents or stick to simpler AI tools, and 38% have no plans to adopt agents at all. Trust is falling: only 29% trust AI output to be accurate, down from 40% in 2024.[7]
  • Digital.ai State of Agile: 84% of agile practitioners report AI adoption (up from 68%) — but only 49% have any governance or guardrails in place. AI is being folded into existing Agile practice, not replaced by a new named lifecycle.
  • Sonar 2026: 42% of committed code is now AI-generated/assisted, but 96% of developers don't fully trust it, and only 48% always verify it before merging.
  • DORA / Google 2025: 90% of developers use AI daily. Individual output rises sharply, but organization-level delivery metrics don't move proportionally — DORA's own framing is that AI is "an amplifier of existing conditions," good or bad, tied to organizational capability rather than to which named methodology (if any) a team adopted.[8]
  • Perforce 2026 State of DevOps: DevOps maturity predicts AI success far better than methodology choice does — 72% of high-maturity organizations report deeply embedded AI, versus 18% of low-maturity ones.
the fair conclusion AI-DLC, Spec Kit, and Agent/works are real, distinct artifacts — not interchangeable, and not fabricated. But the adoption evidence points the same direction across five independent surveys: most teams are integrating agents informally into their existing Scrum/Kanban practice, with governance and trust badly lagging tool adoption, and success correlating with pre-existing engineering maturity rather than which branded lifecycle (if any) they picked. Treat any of these named methodologies as one vendor's or consultancy's packaging of a shift that's mostly happening without a name, not as the industry standard.

Check it sticks

① Name two organizations that deliberately chose not to brand a named development methodology, and what they shipped instead.

Atlassian (principles like "structured intent" and "observable autonomy," plus product features like Jira Planner and Coding Agent, not a ceremony framework) and GitLab (the Duo Agent Platform, embedding agents as platform capabilities rather than a named lifecycle) — both shipped features/principles instead of a branded methodology like AI-DLC.

② What do five independent 2026 developer/DevOps surveys converge on regarding how teams actually adopt AI agents?

Stack Overflow, Digital.ai, Sonar, DORA, and Perforce all show high tool adoption but informal integration into existing practices, weak governance, and success tied to pre-existing DevOps/engineering maturity rather than to adopting any specific named methodology.
