TOOLS & CONCEPTS · LEAN — LESSON 6 OF 7

The AI collision

This is the lesson that answers "why is everyone suddenly talking about Lean?" Short version: language models are good at producing proofs and bad at being trusted. Lean is the opposite.

The 30-second version

A large language model can write a plausible-looking proof — or plausible-looking code — in seconds. The problem is "plausible-looking": the model can hallucinate, meaning produce confident output that is simply wrong, and a subtly wrong proof is worthless. Checking it by hand is slow, expert work. Lean removes that bottleneck. If the model writes its proof in Lean, the kernel (lesson 2) either accepts it or rejects it, and nobody has to read it to know which. An untrusted, tireless generator paired with a cheap, strict checker turns out to be an extremely productive combination — and from 2024 on, a lot of money and attention followed.

It's the same idea as the end of lesson 5 — you don't have to trust the contributor, because the checker verifies the work — with the contributor swapped for a machine.

Why the pairing works

Language modelLean
Coming up with a proofFast, creative, tirelessCan't — it only checks
Being rightNo guarantee at allGuaranteed, relative to a small kernel
When it failsFails silently — wrong answers look like right onesFails loudly — a precise error at a precise line

The third row matters as much as the second. A rejected proof comes back with a specific error message, which the model can read and try again. That makes a loop: generate, check, read the error, repair, repeat — thousands of times, unattended, with no human in the loop until the checker says yes. Any engineer who has watched a coding agent iterate against a test suite knows this loop; the difference is that a passing proof means "correct for all inputs", not "correct for the cases someone thought to test".

In the words of Leonardo de Moura, Lean's creator: "The piece of Lean you actually have to trust is tiny."[1]

What happened, in order

July 2024 — AlphaProof, silver-medal level
Google DeepMind's system scores 28 of 42 on that year's International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO), the hardest high-school math competition — one point short of gold. It works in Lean: humans translated the problems into Lean by hand, and the system searched for proofs the kernel would accept, taking up to three days on a problem.[2] This is the moment the wider AI world noticed Lean.
July 2025 — IMO gold, two different ways
Four systems reach gold-medal level, each solving 5 of 6 problems. OpenAI's and Google's (Gemini Deep Think) wrote proofs in plain English, graded by humans.[3][4] Harmonic's "Aristotle" and ByteDance's "Seed-Prover" wrote proofs in Lean — proofs that, as Harmonic put it, "did not require human checking since they were formally verified".[5][6] Worth noticing: Lean wasn't required to solve the problems. What it removed was the need for graders.
Feb–Mar 2026 — an AI finishes sphere packing
The human project from lesson 5 had been running for two years. Math, Inc.'s system "Gauss" proved the remaining results for the 8-dimensional case in five days, then formalized the 24-dimensional case in two weeks.[7][8]
May 2026 — open problems start to fall
A DeepMind agent resolves 9 of 353 open Erdős problems (a famous collection of unsolved questions) and 44 of 492 open conjectures from an integer-sequence database, all Lean-verified, at a reported cost of a few hundred dollars per problem.[9] A small percentage — but these are new results, not exam questions with known answers.
September 4, 2026 — Fermat's Last Theorem, in 11 days
Anthropic announces what it calls "the first end-to-end, computer-checked proof" of Fermat's Last Theorem: 13 million lines of Lean, produced by a group of AI agents "in 11 days, working largely autonomously".[10] This is the theorem Kevin Buzzard's five-year human project (lesson 5) was funded to formalize. Buzzard's post the same day is titled "FLT: Anthropic has beaten me to it". He downloaded it and checked it himself — "it checks out" — and added, dryly: "I was given £1M to run my project over 5 years; Anthropic took only 11 days but I do wonder if they spent more money…"[11]
read this result correctly The FLT formalization contains no new mathematics, and both sides say so. Anthropic: "what's novel here is the verification".[10] Buzzard: mathematically it "tells us essentially nothing" — it follows a proof humans wrote in the 1990s.[11] What it shows is how cheap formalizing known mathematics has become. Lesson 7 is entirely about reading results like this without over- or under-reacting.

The money

A group of startups now exists whose whole product is "AI that proves things in Lean". Harmonic raised $120M at a $1.45B valuation in November 2025.[12] as of Nov 2026 Math, Inc. (Gauss) and Axiom are in the same space. This is a large part of why Lean shows up in tech news rather than only in math departments.

Why a software engineer should care

Everything above is about mathematics. But recall from lesson 1 that Lean is also a programming language, and that "this function is correct" is a theorem like any other. The same loop applies to code — and code is where AI generation is already outrunning human review.

Martin Kleppmann (author of Designing Data-Intensive Applications) made the prediction in December 2025: "AI will bring formal verification, which for decades has been a bit of a fringe pursuit, into the software engineering mainstream."[13] His reasoning has two halves. Verification was always too expensive because proofs took scarce experts months — AI makes writing the proofs cheap. And AI-written code makes verification more wanted: "I'd much rather have the AI prove to me that the code it has generated is correct" than review it line by line.[13]

De Moura makes the same argument more bluntly: "It will get worse, and it will get worse fast, unless verification scales with generation."[14] (He is Lean's architect, so read that as advocacy — but it's advocacy with deployments behind it.) Amazon's Cedar, the language behind AWS authorization policies, keeps a Lean model of its core with proofs about it, and "no new Cedar version is released unless its model, proofs, and differential tests are current."[15] And the math-AI companies are following the money toward software: during autumn 2026 Harmonic announced verification for C, Verilog, and Rust.[16]

Check it sticks

① Explain to a colleague, in two or three sentences, why LLMs and Lean fit together.

An LLM can generate proofs (or code) quickly but can't be trusted — it may be confidently wrong, and checking by hand is slow. Lean can't generate anything but checks mechanically, and rejects with a precise error. Put together you get a loop — generate, check, repair — that runs unattended and only stops on output that is actually verified, so nobody has to trust the model.

② At IMO 2025, some gold-level AI systems answered in plain English and some in Lean. What did the Lean-based ones gain?

All four solved the same 5 of 6. The natural-language systems needed expert humans to grade the proofs; the Lean ones arrived already verified. Lean's contribution isn't raw problem-solving power — it's removing the trust step.

③ What is the right one-line reading of the September 2026 Fermat's Last Theorem result?

The agents followed a published 1990s exposition of the existing proof. The news is the cost collapse: a five-year, £1M human formalization target was reached in 11 days. Both Anthropic and Buzzard are explicit that no new mathematics came out of it.
