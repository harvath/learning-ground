Fifty years before Lean

Nothing in lesson 2 was new in 2013. Proof assistants had existed since the 1960s and had real triumphs. This lesson is about what they achieved — and the three things that kept them a specialist craft.

The 30-second version

By the time Lean started, machine-checked proof was a proven technology. Older proof assistants had verified famous theorems, a C compiler, and an operating-system kernel. But each success cost years of expert labor, the results lived in separate libraries that didn't fit together, and the tools were built by computer scientists for computer scientists — most mathematicians never touched them. The technology worked. The ecosystem didn't exist.

The family tree, briefly

1967 — Automath
N.G. de Bruijn starts a project aiming at "a system for the mechanic verification of mathematics" — one of the very first, and the source of the small-trusted-checker principle from lesson 2.[1]
1986 — Isabelle
From Lawrence Paulson at Cambridge. Descends from a different 1970s line of systems, and uses a simpler logic without dependent types.[1][2] A close relative, HOL Light, appears below.
1989 — Coq
From France's INRIA; implementation began in 1984, first released 1989.[3] Built on dependent type theory and propositions-as-types — the same foundation Lean would later use. Coq is Lean's closest ancestor. (It was renamed Rocq in March 2025,[4] so you'll see both names.)

Three reasons it stayed niche

1. The cost

At person-years per result, formal proof made sense only where a bug was catastrophic (aircraft software, a kernel) or a proof was famous and in doubt (Kepler). Nobody formalized ordinary work.

2. Libraries that didn't add up

Each big project built the mathematics it needed, in its own style, for its own purposes. A 2020 Quanta article describes Coq's libraries as built by individuals for their own projects — decentralized and unorganized — and quotes Coq developer Assia Mahboubi: "Coq is an old man now, and it has a lot of scars."[10] For a programmer, picture an ecosystem with no standard library and no package manager, where every project re-implements strings and lists, incompatibly. Each success left little for the next one to stand on.

3. The wrong audience

The tools came out of computer science and logic, and it showed. Kevin Buzzard — a number theorist who'll be a central figure in lesson 6 — wrote in 2020 that the existing systems had formalized mostly "undergraduate-level mathematical objects", that "the learning curve is too high and much of the documentation is written for computer scientists", and asked why none of the mathematics his colleagues actually worked on could even be stated in them.[11] Through the 2010s, proof assistants were, as Quanta later put it, "an intriguing subplot in mathematics".[12]

the takeaway When people call Lean "new", the logic is not what they mean — lesson 2's ideas date from the 1930s to the 1980s, and Coq had implemented them for decades. What was missing was cheaper proofs, one shared library, and the mathematicians. Those are engineering and community problems, and that is where Lean made its difference.

Check it sticks

① Name the three things that kept proof assistants niche before Lean.

(1) Cost — each result took person-years of expert effort. (2) Fragmented libraries — every project built its own mathematics, so work didn't accumulate. (3) Audience — the tools were built by and for computer scientists; working mathematicians found them hard to learn and unable to express the mathematics they cared about.

② Which older system is Lean's closest ancestor, sharing its foundation of dependent types and propositions-as-types?

Coq is built on the Calculus of Constructions, the same family of type theory Lean uses. Isabelle and HOL Light come from a different lineage with a simpler logic and no dependent types.

③ The seL4 kernel took about 2.2 person-years to build. Roughly how much effort did its correctness proof take?

Around ten times the cost of the code itself. That ratio is the economic fact behind the whole pre-Lean era — and the number that lesson 7's AI story is about to attack.
