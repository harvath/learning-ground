The "don't sound behind the times" list

Units A and B taught the current landscape. This lesson is about recognizing when someone (possibly a future version of this course) is describing an old one.

The original agent hype wave: AutoGPT

Early 2023's AutoGPT, BabyAGI, and AgentGPT were the first mainstream attempt at fully autonomous, self-directed AI agents — and the first mainstream disappointment: they reliably got "stuck in loops or produced nonsense plans." They're a historical reference point now, not a live product category — if someone cites AutoGPT as evidence of what agents can do today, they're two full agent-tooling generations behind Unit B's actual landscape.

"Prompt engineer" as a job title: dead

Job postings with the exact title "Prompt Engineer" cratered roughly 40% between mid-2024 and early 2025, and are now near-zero in most markets. The underlying skill didn't disappear — demand for it roughly tripled from 2024 to 2026 — it just stopped being a standalone job and got absorbed into broader AI/agent engineering roles (A5's "naming chaos" section covers what replaced it). Frontier models simply got robust enough that careful, expert-crafted prompts stopped being the differentiator vague natural-language requests couldn't match.

The next handoff: "loop engineering" (June 2026)

If prompt engineering is already dated, the term that's displacing it in mid-2026 discourse is loop engineering — and it's recent enough that it's worth catching while it's still live rather than looking it up cold in conversation. On June 7, 2026, Peter Steinberger — OpenClaw's creator, now at OpenAI after the acquihire (A2) — posted a line that reached 6.5M views: "You shouldn't be prompting coding agents anymore. You should be designing loops that prompt your agents." Boris Cherny, who leads Claude Code at Anthropic, said essentially the same thing independently: "I don't prompt Claude anymore. I have loops running that prompt Claude and figuring out what to do. My job is to write loops." Two weeks later, Addy Osmani (Google Chrome engineering lead) published the essay that gave the idea a name and a structure.[1]

the actual distinction Prompt engineering is a single-turn skill: write a prompt, read the output, write the next prompt yourself. Loop engineering is a systems-design skill one level up — you build the thing that finds work, hands it to the agent, verifies the result, records progress, and decides what to run next, so the agent iterates for minutes or hours without you typing each step. Osmani's own framing: "Loop engineering is replacing yourself as the person who prompts the agent. You design the system that does it instead."[1] His essay names five concrete components of a loop — scheduled automations, isolated worktrees for parallel runs, codified SKILL.md project knowledge, MCP plugins/connectors, and subagents for independent verification — plus external state (markdown files or a board) so progress survives between runs.

Same arc as "prompt engineer" above, one turn further: as agents got capable enough to run unattended for hours, the bottleneck moved from "can you phrase the instruction well" to "can you design the system that keeps re-instructing it correctly." Whether "loop engineer" becomes an actual job title or, like prompt engineering, gets folded into general agent-engineering practice within a year, is exactly the kind of thing worth checking rather than assuming — see C2 for how to keep this current.

The DeepSeek moment — and what actually changed

On January 20, 2025, DeepSeek released R1, an o1-class reasoning model, fully open-weight, at a fraction of the training cost OpenAI and others had assumed necessary. A week later, on January 27, Nvidia lost about 17% of its market cap — roughly $600B — in a single day, the largest one-day market-cap loss by any public company ever, on fears that efficient open models would crater GPU demand.[2]

the stock move wasn't the lasting story Nvidia's sell-off fully reversed within about a month — a $1,000 investment made at the panic's low point was worth roughly $1,480 by mid-2026. What actually stuck wasn't the market wobble; it was cementing "open-weight models can credibly chase the frontier at much lower cost" as a permanent, ongoing feature of the landscape (DeepSeek's V4, mid-2026, continues the same pattern — B7's discussion of the open-model ecosystem sits downstream of this moment). If someone frames DeepSeek's efficiency claims as a one-off surprise rather than an established, recurring pattern, that's a sign they haven't updated since early 2025.

"Vibe coding" — a term that's drifted from its own coinage

Andrej Karpathy (A4) coined "vibe coding" in a viral post on February 2, 2025 — a specific practice of fully giving in to AI-generated code without reading diffs, iterating purely by pasting error messages back. It went from tweet to Collins Dictionary's 2025 Word of the Year within months. By 2026 the term has diluted: it's now used loosely for any AI-assisted coding at all, has picked up a mildly negative connotation, and is specifically frowned on as a description of serious production work. Someone using it uncritically as a positive description of professional engineering — rather than Karpathy's original, narrower, more reckless meaning — is a small tell they picked the term up secondhand rather than from its origin.

chip export controls: a genuine policy whiplash Biden-era rules banned H20 chip exports to China (April 2025) → the Trump administration reversed course, allowing H200 sales (announced Dec 2025, codified with a 25% tariff and volume cap in Jan 2026) → but as of mid-2026, essentially zero H200 chips have actually shipped to China, because Beijing itself is blocking the imports in favor of its own Huawei-based domestic stack. The story flipped from "will the US allow this" (a US policy question) to "will China accept it" (a Chinese policy question) — a shift easy to miss if you last checked in on this story around the original ban.

Check it sticks

① What actually changed permanently after the January 2025 DeepSeek R1 moment, versus what was just a temporary market reaction?

Nvidia's ~17% single-day stock drop was temporary — it fully reversed within about a month. What stuck permanently was the establishment of "open-weight models can credibly chase the frontier at a fraction of the cost" as an ongoing feature of the landscape, not a one-off surprise — a pattern that's continued through DeepSeek's later releases.

② "Loop engineering" and "prompt engineering" both involve talking to an AI agent. What's the actual difference in what you're doing?

Prompt engineering is single-turn: you write a prompt, read the result, and write the next prompt yourself. Loop engineering is a systems-design skill one level up: you build the automated system — scheduling, verification, state-tracking — that finds work, prompts the agent, checks the result, and decides what to run next, so the agent iterates for minutes or hours without you typing each step.

③ Someone describes "vibe coding" today as a positive term for professional, production-grade software engineering. What does that suggest?

Karpathy's original 2025 meaning was specific and a little reckless (fully trusting AI output without reading diffs). By 2026 the term broadened into general AI-assisted coding and picked up a negative connotation for production use — using it uncritically as praise for serious engineering is a small, specific tell of outdated usage.
