UNIT A · THE ECONOMIC STRUCTURE — LESSON 0005

The labor market is being pulled apart in two directions at once

Behind every name in A4 is a hiring story. This lesson is that story at the population level — and it's genuinely two contradictory stories happening simultaneously.

Forward Deployed Engineers — the role spreading fastest

The Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role originated at Palantir: engineers embedded directly with a customer, building bespoke solutions on Palantir's platform, then feeding what they learn back into the core product. By 2026, it's spread far beyond Palantir — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Databricks, and Cohere have all adopted some version of it, and in a single two-week window in May 2026, ServiceNow (with partners Accenture and Cognizant), Anthropic, and OpenAI all formalized their own FDE structures.[1] What distinguishes it from a standard software-engineer or solutions-engineer role: it's customer-embedded, it builds in production against real (often messy) enterprise data, and it sits closer to sales/delivery than to core product engineering.

The investment scale is real, not just headcount: Anthropic reportedly structured a $1.5B joint venture around this model; OpenAI is investing roughly $10B; SAP committed €100M to a partner fund pushing its own agent products into customer environments the same way. FDE job postings jumped over 800% between January and September 2025 alone.[2] Compensation figures vary a lot by source (these are recruiting-aggregator estimates, not company-disclosed numbers, so treat them as directional): frontier-lab FDEs reportedly range from ~$385K at mid-level to $1M+ at principal level, with equity now making up 55–70% of total comp at the top of the market, up from 35–45% in 2024.[3]

Forty titles, three real jobs

FDE isn't the only new title. "AI Engineer" has been the single fastest-growing job title in the US for two consecutive years. Context Engineer has gone from buzzword to an actual hired title — Adobe posted an explicit "AI Context Engineer" role in April 2026, paying roughly $140K–$230K.[4] Alongside these: Agent Engineer, Agentic AI Engineer, Agent Architect, AgentOps — a proliferating set of titles with real postings but a lot of overlap.

a healthy dose of skepticism One 2026 commentator (Ivan Turković, a CTO writing in April 2026) counted roughly 40 distinct AI job titles currently in use — and argued they map to only about 3 real underlying jobs: (1) API-based product builders — by far the largest group, ~80% of the market — people building product features by calling someone else's already-trained model (RAG pipelines, evals, product glue code), the real work behind titles like Prompt Engineer, RAG Engineer, Context Engineer, and Agent Engineer, median $159K–$245K; (2) model training specialists — actually training or fine-tuning models (ML Engineer, ML Research Engineer, Research Scientist), the older, often PhD-heavy discipline, median ~$265K; and (3) infrastructure & operations — keeping the AI systems running (MLOps, LLMOps, AI Platform/Infrastructure roles), median ~$161K.[5] This is worth holding onto as a default posture: when a new title shows up in a job posting or a LinkedIn headline, the useful question isn't "what does this title mean" so much as "which of these three it's actually describing" — title inflation is itself a symptom of a market moving faster than HR departments can standardize vocabulary, not evidence of genuinely new work every time.

Mercor — matching people to this economy, and feeding it

Mercor, founded in 2023 by three engineers in their early twenties (Brendan Foody, Surya Midha, Adarsh H.), does AI-driven candidate sourcing, vetting, and onboarding — but its more distinctive business is supplying human domain experts into AI labs' own training pipelines (RLHF, evaluation, and similar work), not just placing candidates into ordinary jobs.[6] Its funding trajectory tracks the broader frenzy: $100M at a $2B valuation in February 2025, $350M at $10B in October 2025, and — as of July 2026 — reportedly in talks to raise $500M at a $20B valuation, doubling in about nine months.[7] Worth sitting with: a company whose core product is finding and vetting the human labor that trains AI models is itself one of the fastest-appreciating companies in the AI economy — a nice, concrete illustration that "the AI economy" still runs on human labor underneath, just relocated.

two things happening at once AI was the stated reason for 38,579 job cuts in May 2026 alone — 40% of all May tech layoffs, up sharply from just 7% in January 2026. Year-to-date AI-attributed cuts (~87,714) already exceed all of 2025's total.[8] Named companies: Oracle (30,000), Amazon (16,000), Meta (8,000), IBM (7,800). This is happening in the exact same months that the hyperscalers committed roughly $700B in 2026 capex — nearly double 2025 — explicitly framed by several outlets as "cutting headcount to fund AI infrastructure." Simultaneously, AI-specialist compensation keeps climbing: a reported ~28% premium over general tech roles, with a Senior ML Engineer at a frontier lab earning roughly $600K+ against ~$220K for the same title at a typical enterprise.[9] Same technology, same year, same industry — destroying a large number of jobs and inflating pay for a narrow specialist class, at the same time. That tension, not either half alone, is the honest picture of this labor market right now.

Check it sticks

① What makes a Forward Deployed Engineer role different from a standard software engineer or solutions engineer role?

It's customer-embedded (works directly inside a specific client's environment, not on the core product team), builds bespoke solutions in production against real enterprise data, and sits closer to sales/delivery than to core product engineering — while still feeding what's learned back into the vendor's core product.

② What's the most defensible response to encountering a new, unfamiliar AI job title in 2026?

With roughly 40 distinct AI job titles estimated to map to only about 3 real underlying jobs, the useful skeptical move is asking what real work sits underneath a new title — not assuming novelty, and not dismissing it outright either, since some of the 40 do describe real, distinct work.
