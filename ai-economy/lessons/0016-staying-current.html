UNIT C · READING THE DISCOURSE — LESSON 0016

The habit, not just the reading list

Sixteen lessons of facts will start decaying the moment this course stops being updated. This closing lesson is the part meant to outlast all of them.

Every lesson in this course carried an implicit discipline: treat specific numbers, names, and product claims as dated, cite them, and expect them to need revision. That discipline is the actual deliverable — not any single fact in Units A or B, all of which will be partly wrong within months. This lesson turns it into something repeatable.

Where to check

The full curated list lives in this workspace's RESOURCES.md, but the shape is worth internalizing directly:

  • For the economic layer (Unit A): Stratechery for structural analysis of why a deal matters, not just that it happened; The Information for deal/valuation scoops; CB Insights and Epoch AI for the underlying quantitative data (funding, compute, training cost); Stanford HAI's annual AI Index as the once-a-year full reset.
  • For the technology layer (Unit B): a project's own docs/blog/GitHub release notes are always the ground truth over secondhand summaries — this course cited primary sources (Anthropic's own MCP announcement, Palantir's own Ontology docs, AWS's own AI-DLC posts) specifically so you could go verify or update them directly.
  • For ongoing signal, not just facts: Latent Space and Interconnects for what builders are actually shipping; Dwarkesh's long-form interviews for primary-source access to the people building frontier models; r/LocalLLaMA as a sentiment/reality-check supplement, not a primary source.

A repeatable check, not a one-time read

Pick two or three sources from the list above — not all of them — and check in on a cadence, quarterly is reasonable given how this course's own research showed things moving. Specifically look for four patterns this course kept surfacing:

  1. New funding rounds or valuations that make A6's numbers stale (they will — A1's OpenAI/Anthropic figures were already described as "the fastest-decaying facts in this report" when first written).
  2. New departures, hires, or mergers among A4/A5's names and B4's frameworks (Semantic Kernel+AutoGen merging into Microsoft Agent Framework is exactly this pattern — expect more).
  3. A "X is dead, Y replaced it" story — C1's whole lesson is built from these; there will be a next one.
  4. A vendor's own claim about its methodology or product being adopted — B6 taught the specific skill of checking whether that claim is independently verified or vendor-sourced only. Apply it every time.

Check it sticks

① What are the four patterns worth specifically watching for when you check back on a source, based on what this course kept running into?

New funding rounds/valuations that make earlier numbers stale; new departures/hires/mergers among the people and frameworks covered; "X is dead, Y replaced it" narrative shifts; and vendor claims about adoption/methodology that need checking for independent verification versus vendor-only sourcing.

Every unit demonstrated the same discipline applied to a different domain — reading claims critically (A7), distinguishing settled from contested terms (B5), recognizing dated narratives (C1). The specific facts will decay; the habit of checking, citing, and expecting revision is what's meant to stick.
