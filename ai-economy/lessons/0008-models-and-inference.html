Training, inference, and the word that means three things

Unit A covered who's in the industry. Unit B covers what's actually being built — starting from the bottom of the stack, since everything else (B2–B7) sits on top of this.

A model is, mechanically, a very large set of numbers (parameters) tuned so that feeding text in produces useful text out. Two distinct phases matter here, and confusing them causes real misreadings of headlines: training is the process of learning those parameters from data — expensive, slow, done rarely, and the thing labs spend their enormous compute budgets on (A6). Inference is running the already-trained model to actually produce an output — cheap per-request by comparison, done constantly, and the thing every product built on a model (Unit A's whole tooling layer) actually pays for on an ongoing basis.

"Inference" — one word, three senses

You'll see "inference" used loosely across three genuinely different things. Worth disambiguating explicitly, since conflating them causes real confusion:

(a) The base ML sense — running a trained model to produce output, as opposed to training it. This is the definition above, and every other sense below builds on it.

(b) Hugging Face's Inference Providers (B7 goes deep on this) — a marketplace/router giving pay-per-use access to 15+ hosted-inference partners (Groq, Together AI, Fireworks, Cerebras, and others) through one unified API. Hugging Face takes no markup on this — it's a funnel to keep developer attention in its ecosystem, not a direct profit center.[1]

(c) Inference-time / test-time compute — reasoning models spending extra computation at answer time (extended "thinking") to improve accuracy on hard problems, popularized by OpenAI's o1 (Sept 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (Jan 2025). A reasoning-model query typically costs 30–100x the tokens of a normal chat query — and the technique's cost-efficiency depends heavily on the specific model, not just "spend more, get more": DeepSeek-R1 delivered comparable reasoning quality to o1 at roughly 27x cheaper API pricing at the time.[2]

why this distinction is worth the effort "Inference costs are falling" and "inference-time compute is rising" are both true statements in 2026, about different things, and people run them together constantly. (a)/(b) — the cost of running a fixed-size model per request — has generally been falling as hardware and serving techniques improve. (c) — a newer technique of deliberately spending more compute per request to reason longer — pushes the other direction for the specific queries that use it. A headline about "inference costs" without specifying which of these it means is not fully readable yet.

Check it sticks

① What's the difference between training and inference, and which one do A1's "labs burning cash" figures mostly reflect?

Training is the expensive, infrequent process of learning a model's parameters from data. Inference is running the already-trained model to produce output, done constantly and comparatively cheap per request. Labs' huge compute spending (A6) is mostly training cost — the enormous one-time-ish investment behind each new model generation — plus the serving infrastructure for inference at massive scale.

② A reasoning model spending extra tokens "thinking" before answering a hard question is an example of which sense of "inference"?

All reasoning-model "thinking" happens during inference in the base sense too, but the specific named phenomenon — spending extra compute at answer-time to trade latency/cost for accuracy — is inference-time (test-time) compute, popularized by o1 and DeepSeek-R1.
