Three definitions, one phrase — pick the wrong one and you'll misjudge every SDV claim you hear

"Software-defined vehicle" gets used by automakers, consultants, and journalists as if it means one settled thing. It doesn't. Before you can place any company's claim into context, you need to know which definition they're implicitly using — because "is this an SDV?" gets a different answer depending on which one you pick.

Three definitions, compared

McKinseyStep-change / binaryCentralized E/E architecture, OTA updates, and hardware/software developed on decoupled timelines[2]
Gartner (Mike Ramsey)Binary, capability-basedFeature upgrades happen via software, not by swapping physical parts[1]
PwCSix-level maturity model (0–5)"Full SDV" is Level 4: zonal architecture, standardized APIs, cloud-based development, frequent OTA. Level 5 adds a third-party app ecosystem[3]
Bosch (Stefan Buerkle)Hard technical thresholdECU count consolidated down to one or two central computers, from a prior 100+[1]

McKinsey and Gartner both treat "software-defined" as a step change: a vehicle either has the decoupled, centralized architecture or it doesn't. PwC treats it as a continuum — almost every modern car already sits somewhere on a ladder explicitly modeled on SAE's autonomous-driving levels, climbing from Level 0 (mechanically controlled) toward Level 5 (a fully cloud-connected software-defined ecosystem).[3] That difference isn't academic: if a client asks "are we SDV-ready?", McKinsey's framework might answer "no, not yet," while PwC's answers "you're already partway there." Neither is wrong — they're different ladders, and you need to know which one is being climbed before the answer means anything.

Where the term came from — and why nobody quite owns it

The common story is that Tesla coined "software-defined vehicle" around 2012, borrowing the phrase from "software-defined radio" as it centralized vehicle functions in the Model S. That story is repeated widely — but it traces back to secondary and tertiary write-ups, not to a primary document. TechCrunch's January 2024 explainer, a considerably higher-trust source, says the opposite directly: no single company or person is credited with popularizing the term, which instead gained traction gradually through the mid-to-late 2010s as multiple analysts converged on similar language.[1]

What is solidly documented, and worth keeping separate from the coinage question: Tesla shipped its first over-the-air software update — version 1.9.11, to more than 100 Model S owners — in late 2012.[5] That's a real, dated fact. "And that's the moment the term was coined" is folk history layered on top of it, and it doesn't survive a primary-source check. Treat it as contested rather than settled — a habit worth carrying through the rest of this course, since several other widely repeated SDV facts turn out to have the same problem.

handle with care When someone tells you a vehicle is or isn't "SDV," ask which of the four framings above they're using. A car that's "not SDV" under Bosch's ECU-consolidation threshold might already be "Level 3: Partial SDV" under PwC's ladder. This isn't pedantry — in a client conversation, conflating the two can make you sound like you're contradicting a source that was actually using a different yardstick entirely.
